## Personal blog draft

Title: Why I’m building a portfolio people can inspect

Suggested slug: learning-with-evidence

I’m building practical Salesforce and CRM skills through self-directed projects. The projects use fictional scenarios, and I want their status to be easy to understand. That means showing the thinking as well as the result.

A useful project starts with a question. What information needs to be captured? Who should be able to see it? What happens when a person changes roles? A fictional scenario gives me a way to explore those questions without presenting the work as a paid client engagement.

The next step is documenting decisions. A record of what I chose and why helps me revisit the work later. It also gives someone reviewing the portfolio a way to understand the assumptions. If a decision changes, the explanation should change with it.

Checks matter too. I want to distinguish between a feature that has been implemented, a check that was actually performed and an idea that remains in planning. Where something is unfinished, I aim to say so clearly. Evidence should support the claim being made, rather than make an incomplete project look complete.

My customer-facing operations background shapes the questions I ask. Can someone understand the information? Is the next action clear? Is the handover practical? Those questions are useful alongside technical learning.

## Business blog draft

Title: Start an enquiry workflow with three clear questions

Suggested slug: clearer-enquiry-workflow

A new enquiry is the start of a process. Before exploring automation, I would begin by asking where the information is captured, who owns the next action and how follow-up is recorded. These questions help frame a conversation about a workflow without assuming a particular tool will solve it.

The first stage is capture. In a fictional trade-business scenario, an enquiry might arrive with a name, a service request and some context. The useful question is what information is actually needed for the next step. A demo can explore that structure without using real customer information.

The second stage is organisation. Once the enquiry exists, the workflow needs a visible status and someone responsible for progressing it. For a demonstration, that might mean recording that a request needs review and assigning a fictional owner. The details should reflect the scenario rather than be presented as a universal formula.

The third stage is follow-up. What is the next action, and how will someone know whether it happened? A fictional workflow can explore notes and next steps. It should also make clear which parts have been implemented and which remain ideas.

Automation becomes a more useful discussion when the underlying process is understood. A demo is a way to test assumptions and document limitations. It does not establish that a live business will achieve a particular saving or outcome.
